# Humanize and Tighten

Edit like a thoughtful colleague: say what happened, why it matters and what the reader needs to understand it. Aim for natural, direct prose rather than a conspicuously polished or formulaic voice.

Use this skill on its own. When paired with `plain-english-with-context`, use that skill for clear explanation and context, then use this one as an editing pass. Neither skill requires the other.

## Make the text easier to read

- Identify the reader's question and put the answer or main finding first. Preserve the author's intent and requested scope.
- Replace abstract labels with concrete actions. Prefer "We checked 100 tickets" to "An assessment was conducted across the ticket population."
- Use familiar words, active verbs and natural sentence lengths. Keep technical terms when they are useful, and briefly explain unfamiliar ones.
- Remove repeated explanations, throat-clearing, canned transitions, promotional claims and sentences that merely announce the next sentence. Avoid forced informality, invented anecdotes and unnecessary adjectives.
- Let each paragraph develop one point. Use bullets for parallel items and tables for comparisons. Add headings only when they help navigation; do not impose the same template on every draft.
- Keep the examples and definitions that make the result understandable. Shorten wording before cutting useful context. Do not aim for a fixed word count or reduction percentage.

## Keep qualifications useful

A qualification earns its place when removing it would change what the reader believes or how they should use the result. Keep material distinctions such as an estimate versus a direct count, a sample versus a whole population, or an unknown executor versus a confirmed human action.

State each necessary qualification once, close to the result it explains. Combine overlapping caveats. Put detailed method notes in an appendix when the requested format supports one; keep essential interpretation beside the numbers. Remove generic warnings and repeated lists of everything a result does not prove.

Preserve the strength of the evidence. Do not turn a recorded report into independent verification, an association into a cause, or missing evidence into proof that something did not happen. Simplify uncertainty without erasing it.

## Check the edit

- Preserve names, dates, units, counts, denominators, links and the meaning of table entries unless the user asks to change them. Fix a factual error only when the supplied evidence supports the correction; flag unresolved contradictions briefly.
- Check that the reader can identify the finding, its meaning and any limitation that changes its use without rereading.
- Return the revised text by default. Add a short change note only when useful or requested; avoid appending another summary that repeats the text.

## Example

Before:

> Across the sample of 100 reviewed tickets, the agent was attributed 60% of the recorded actions. It is important to note that these results do not necessarily represent the entire population. Additionally, the analysis does not establish who performed the remaining actions. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

After:

> In the 100 tickets we reviewed, the agent completed 60% of the recorded actions. We could not identify who performed the rest.

The sample and unknown attribution remain clear without repeating generic warnings.
